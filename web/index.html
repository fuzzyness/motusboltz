How heat turns a wheel

Below is a working engine made of 2,048 helium particles. One cylinder touches a hot wall and the other a cold one. Nothing pushes the pistons except particles bouncing off them, yet the wheel keeps turning. This page explains why, one law of thermodynamics at a time.

Run the engine

This is a model of a real machine, the Stirling engine, patented by the Scottish minister Robert Stirling in 1816. Ours has two cylinders side by side, joined by an opening low in the wall between them. Gas moves back and forth through it while two pistons, linked to one wheel, rise and fall.

Start it in four steps

  1. Click the engine, so your keys go to it.
  2. Drag Hot side up and Cold side down, with the sliders on the right. Any big difference works, and the bigger it is, the faster the engine runs: 1200 K and 100 K is the fastest. The flames around the hot cylinder and the ice around the cold one grow with each setting.
  3. Press Space or the Start button under the engine. This gives the wheel a single push to get it moving. From then on, only the gas keeps it going, and the button turns into Pause until the wheel stops again.
  4. Watch for a minute or two. One full turn takes about 16 seconds, and the readings for each law appear in the panel on the left after the first full turn.

The flames and the ice stand for the hot and cold sides, which are part of the surroundings, not the gas. A real Stirling engine is heated by a burner, the sun or waste heat, and cooled by water or air, rather than by open flames and ice.

What the colors mean

Each dot is colored by how fast it moves.

  • Blue: slower than an average particle at room temperature
  • Orange: about average for 300 K (27 °C)
  • Red: about three times the energy of an average room-temperature particle, or more

A dot flashes white for a moment when it collides with another one.

Press F or Tracers to follow five dots, some starting in each cylinder. They are drawn larger, the rest dim, and a fading yellow line shows each one's path. With the sliders apart, watch one leave the hot wall faster and redder, or the cold wall slower and bluer.

Reading the panel

Simple
The gas temperatures and the wheel's speed right now, a diagram of where the energy goes each turn (the helium is the system, everything it trades energy with is its surroundings), and one card per law with the engine's own numbers. Orange is heat in, yellow is work and blue is heat out.
Advanced
Every reading: the gas in each cylinder, the flywheel and its load, the exact equation behind each law, a pressure against volume graph for each cylinder (the area inside each loop is the work done in that turn), and energy checks.
Both
Per-turn numbers average the last full turns, up to three. Changing a temperature starts the average over. Use the buttons above the engine to show or hide the panel and the list of keys.

Words to know

Each term is tied to something you can watch happen in the engine.

System and surroundings

The system is whatever you are studying. Everything else is the surroundings.

The system is the helium gas. The hot wall and its flames, the cold wall and its ice, the pistons and the wheel are all surroundings.

In a pot of soup, the soup is the system. The pot, the stove and the kitchen are the surroundings.

Temperature

A measure of how fast the particles move on average. Hotter means faster.

Dots in the hot cylinder turn orange and red, and dots in the cold cylinder turn blue. A single particle has a speed, not a temperature: temperature describes the whole crowd.

Internal energy (U)

All the energy stored in the system. Helium atoms only have energy of motion, so for this gas U is the total kinetic energy of every particle.

The panel's dU is how much the gas's energy changed over a turn. Once the engine settles into a steady rhythm, it stays close to zero.

Heat (q)

Energy that moves because of a temperature difference. On its own, heat always flows from hotter to colder.

A particle that hits the hot wall bounces off faster, so heat flowed into the gas. One that hits the cold wall bounces off slower, so heat flowed out.

Work (w)

Energy moved by a push that makes something move.

Every particle that hits a piston gives it a tiny push. All those pushes add up to the force that turns the wheel.

State function

A quantity that depends only on where the system is now, not on how it got there. Temperature, pressure, volume and internal energy are state functions. Heat and work are not: they depend on the path taken.

After each steady turn, the gas comes back to about the same temperature and pressure, so U is unchanged. The heat and work totals, though, keep growing turn after turn.

Your height above sea level is a state function. The distance you hiked to get there is not.

Reversible and irreversible

A reversible change could run backward and leave no trace. Real changes are irreversible: they only go one way on their own.

Much of the heat leaks straight through the gas from the hot side to the cold side without doing any work. That leak is irreversible, and it is the main reason this engine turns only a few percent of its heat into work.

An ice cube melts in a glass of warm water. The water never freezes it back on its own.

Entropy (S)

A measure of how spread out energy is, in joules per kelvin (J/K). Energy tends to spread out, so the total entropy of the universe tends to rise.

Heat leaving the hot wall and arriving at the cold wall spreads energy out. The panel's dS surr shows the entropy of the surroundings rising every turn.

A drop of food coloring spreads through a glass of water and never gathers itself back into a drop.

Enthalpy (H)

The heat content of a system at constant pressure. Its change, ΔH, is the heat a reaction takes in (positive, endothermic) or gives off (negative, exothermic).

A hand warmer gives off heat (ΔH < 0). An instant cold pack takes heat in (ΔH > 0).

The engine has no chemical reactions, so it has no ΔH of its own. The heat its walls trade with the gas plays the same part, as the spontaneity section shows.

Spontaneous

A change that happens on its own, without continuous help from outside. It says nothing about speed: iron rusting is spontaneous but slow.

Heat flowing from the hot wall to the cold wall is spontaneous. The engine catches some of it on the way past.

Gibbs free energy (G)

One number that combines enthalpy and entropy to tell whether a change happens on its own at a given temperature: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. A negative ΔG means yes. Try it in the calculator.

Heat engine and efficiency (η)

A heat engine turns part of a flow of heat into work. Its efficiency, η (the Greek letter eta), is the work out divided by the heat in: η = W / Qhot.

The panel shows the efficiency each turn on a bar marked with the Carnot limit: the highest efficiency any engine could reach between the same two temperatures.

The three laws

Thermodynamics rests on a few laws that no experiment has ever broken. Each one shows up in the engine.

1

First law: energy is never created or destroyed

Energy can change form or move from place to place, but the total stays the same. For the gas in the engine, every joule that comes in as heat leaves as heat, leaves as work, or stays in the gas.

Qhot + Qcold = W + ΔU

  • Qhot is heat from the hot wall, positive because it comes in.
  • Qcold is heat to the cold wall, negative because it goes out.
  • W is the work the gas does on the pistons.
  • ΔU is the change in the gas's own energy.

Chemistry books write the same law as ΔU = q + w, where w is work done on the system. The gas does work W on the pistons, so w = −W, and both forms say the same thing.

In the engine

In one run with 2,048 particles at 1200 K and 100 K, the Advanced view showed, per turn:

4996.3 − 4879.9 = 107.6 + 8.8 J

Both sides come to 116.4 J. Open the panel and watch the two sides match, turn after turn.

In everyday life

Pump up a bike tire and the pump gets warm. The work you do squeezing the air raises its internal energy, and some of that leaves as heat through the pump.

2

Second law: heat flows from hot to cold, and entropy rises

Left alone, heat always moves from hotter things to colder things, never the reverse. A heat engine can catch part of that flow as work, but never all of it: some heat must always flow on to the cold side. Put another way, every real process raises the total entropy of the universe.

ΔSsurr = −Qhot / Thot − Qcold / Tcold

This is the entropy change of the surroundings over one turn, shown on the panel as dS surr. The hot wall loses heat at a high temperature, which lowers its entropy a little. The cold wall gains nearly the same heat at a low temperature, which raises its entropy a lot. The total goes up.

In the engine

In the run above, dS surr was +44.6 J/K per turn. The gas ends each turn where it started, so this is also the entropy change of the whole universe.

Efficiency was 2.2%, far below the Carnot limit of 1 − 100/1200 = 91.7%. Most of the heat walked straight through the gas.

Try it: set both reservoirs to 300 K and press Start. With no temperature difference, no heat flows through, and the wheel slows down turn after turn.

In everyday life

A hot drink cools to room temperature, and a cold one warms up. Neither ever goes the other way on its own.

A car engine sends much of its fuel's energy out as heat through the radiator and exhaust. No engine can avoid that loss completely.

3

Third law: absolute zero can never be reached

As a substance gets colder, its entropy falls. A perfect crystal at absolute zero (0 K, or −273.15 °C) would have zero entropy, but no process can cool anything all the way to 0 K.

ηmax = 1 − Tcold / Thot

In the engine

The Carnot limit would reach 100% only if Tcold were 0 K. Since that can't happen, no engine can ever turn all of its heat into work. The panel says so under the Carnot readout. The cold slider stops at 100 K, but even a perfect engine with a colder cold side would fall short of 100%.

In everyday life

Scientists have cooled atoms to less than a billionth of a degree above absolute zero, but never to absolute zero itself.

Spontaneity: ΔH, ΔS and ΔG

A spontaneous change happens on its own. Water runs downhill, and heat flows from hot to cold. In 1824 the French engineer Sadi Carnot compared heat engines to water wheels: the flow is spontaneous, and the wheel catches some of it. Chemists predict which way a change will go with Gibbs free energy.

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

  • ΔH is the heat taken in or given off, in kJ/mol.
  • ΔS is the change in the system's entropy, in J/(mol·K).
  • T is the temperature in kelvins.
  • If ΔG is negative, the change happens on its own. If it is positive, it doesn't, but the reverse change does. If it is zero, both directions balance: equilibrium.

The same law in a beaker and in the engine

The second law says a change happens on its own when the total entropy of the universe goes up. The universe here is the system plus its surroundings:

ΔSuniverse = ΔS + ΔSsurr > 0

A reaction that gives off heat hands it to the surroundings. At constant pressure that heat is −ΔH, so the surroundings' entropy changes by

ΔSsurr = −ΔH / T

That is the same formula the engine's panel uses for each of its walls. Put the two together and multiply by −T:

−TΔSuniverse = ΔH − TΔS = ΔG

So a negative ΔG means exactly the same thing as a rising entropy of the universe. Gibbs free energy is the second law, seen from the system's side.

In the engine, dS surr is positive every steady turn, so running the engine is spontaneous. Making heat flow the other way, from cold to hot, would take outside work. That is what a refrigerator does.

What the model leaves out

A model is useful because it leaves things out. Here is what this one simplifies, so you know which numbers to trust.

  • Each dot stands for a crowd of atoms. At the largest fill, 2,048 dots carry 0.299 mol of helium, so each dot stands for about 9 × 1019 atoms. The dots are drawn far bigger than atoms so you can see them.
  • The engine runs slowly. Motion is slowed down so you can follow single particles, and Slow mode slows it four times more. Times and rpm are in model units, not real seconds.
  • The pressure readings are estimates. The Advanced view's pressures are ideal gas estimates, P = nRT/V, from each cylinder's particles. The P-V loops use the real pushes of particles on the pistons.
  • The two sides can show different pressures. At the largest fill the Advanced view's estimates for the hot and cold sides differ about two to one. The estimate treats the dots as points, so it reads low where they crowd closest, on the cold side. Measuring the real push of the gas on the walls, a planned improvement, will show how much of the difference is real.
  • There is no regenerator. Real Stirling engines store heat in a mesh between the cylinders and reuse it on the next stroke. Stirling's 1816 patent already had one, which he called the economiser. Ours lets that heat go, which keeps its efficiency low.
  • The walls never warm up or cool down. They stay at the slider temperatures no matter how much heat they give or take. Heat crosses only the cylinder walls and floors: the pistons and the opening between the cylinders are insulated.
  • The hot and cold cylinders share a wall. In the model, each side of that wall keeps its own cylinder's temperature. In a real engine, metal touching both sides would carry heat straight across, so real engines keep the hot and cold parts apart or insulate them.
